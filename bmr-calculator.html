Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the amount of energy the body needs while completely at rest in a neutral environment, when digestion is inactive. It is similar to how much fuel a parked car consumes while idling. In this state, energy is used only to maintain essential organs such as the heart, brain, lungs, kidneys, liver, nervous system, muscles, skin, and reproductive organs.

For most people, nearly 70% of daily calorie expenditure is used for basic bodily upkeep. Physical activity contributes about 20%, while digestion of food—known as thermogenesis—accounts for roughly 10%.

BMR Measurement

BMR is measured under strict conditions while a person is awake, fully rested, and free from physical or mental stress. Because basal metabolism forms the largest portion of daily calorie requirements, total daily energy needs are calculated by multiplying BMR by an activity factor ranging from 1.2 to 1.9, depending on activity level.

In practice, BMR is usually estimated using statistical equations rather than direct measurement.

BMR Estimation Equations

The Harris-Benedict Equation was one of the earliest methods used to calculate BMR. It was revised in 1984 to improve accuracy. In 1990, the Mifflin-St Jeor Equation was introduced and has since been shown to be more accurate than the revised Harris-Benedict Equation.

The Katch-McArdle Formula differs by calculating resting daily energy expenditure (RDEE) using lean body mass. This makes it more accurate for lean individuals who know their body fat percentage. For most users, the Mifflin-St Jeor Equation is considered the most reliable.

BMR Formulas

Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

Men: BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5
Women: BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161

Revised Harris-Benedict Equation

Men: BMR = 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A + 88.362
Women: BMR = 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A + 447.593

Katch-McArdle Formula

BMR = 370 + 21.6 × (1 − F) × W

Where:
W = body weight in kilograms
H = height in centimeters
A = age
F = body fat percentage

BMR Variables

Muscle Mass: Aerobic exercises such as running or cycling do not directly increase BMR. Anaerobic exercises like weight lifting increase muscle mass, which raises resting energy expenditure.

Age: BMR decreases with age due to muscle loss and reduced metabolic efficiency.

Genetics: Hereditary traits influence metabolic rate and energy expenditure.

Weather: Cold environments increase BMR as the body expends energy to maintain temperature. Excessive heat also raises BMR due to cooling processes. BMR increases by approximately 7% for every 1.36°F rise in body temperature.

Diet: Small, frequent meals may slightly increase BMR, while starvation can reduce BMR by up to 30%.

Pregnancy & Hormones: Pregnancy raises BMR to support fetal growth. Menopause may increase or decrease BMR depending on hormonal changes.

Supplements: Certain supplements and stimulants, such as caffeine, can temporarily increase BMR.

BMR Tests

Online BMR calculators provide estimates only. For precise results, BMR should be measured by a certified professional using calorimetry devices available at medical clinics and fitness centers.

Resting Metabolic Rate (RMR)

Although often confused with BMR, Resting Metabolic Rate (RMR) measures energy expenditure in a relaxed but not fully inactive state. BMR requires stricter conditions than RMR.

Modern Wisdom

A 2005 meta-analysis study on BMR found that even after accounting for known factors, about 26% of metabolic variation remains unexplained. Therefore, BMR and TDEE calculations are estimates, not exact values.

Tracking daily habits such as diet, exercise, and lifestyle provides better long-term results than relying solely on BMR calculations.
